Limitations of Formal Logic Applied to Natural Language

two case studies from the New Testament

What started out as an attempt to formalize two claims of the New Testament ended in heresy in one case and an unprovable premise in the other. The two claims are as follows:

Claim 1.1: “… I am in the Father and the Father is in me; …” John 14:11

Claim 1.2: “… I am in my Father, and you are in me, and I am in you.” John 14:20

Theorem 1: Let A and B be sets. If A ⊂ B and B ⊂ A then A = B

(*the proof is popular and is therefore omitted)

Application of Theorem 1 to Claim 1.1

Jesus ⊂ Father, and Father ⊂ Jesus ⇒ Jesus = Father = God

comment: This establishes the deity of Jesus logically within the premises of the text

Application of Theorem 1 to Claim 1.2

Jesus ⊂ Father

and Disciples ⊂ Jesus, and Jesus ⊂ Disciples: But this implies by Theorem 1 that Disciples = Jesus

But since Jesus = God, by transitive property, the Disciples = God

The Problem of Claim 1

The application of theorem 1 to claim 1.2 leads to the logical deduction that disciples are also God. But the disciples are creatures. Thus this valid logical deduction produces the heresy known as Pantheism — the claim that creatures share God’s essence. This problem arises from the use of the word in, within the same speech, at the same event, to mean two different things. If it did not mean 2 different things then the disciples would have understood themselves to be equal to god. But they did not (Acts 14:14–15). And since the speaker (Jesus) would not have equivocated with the intention to mislead (James 1:13), and neither could he have intended to mean that he is not God (Philippians 2:6); that means he knew his audience would be able to parse the difference. How so; by context, shared assumption, and theological intuition.

Claim 2

“If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and* you are still in your sins.” 1 Corinthians 15

Note*: the New International Version (NIV) translation of the NT uses a semi-colon instead of the and conjunctive.

Let’s define the following propositions and their negations after:

P = Christ has been raised

Q = Your faith is futile

S = You are still in your sins

∼P = Christ has not been raised

∼Q = Your faith is not futile

∼S = You are not still in your sins

The formal statement of Claim 2 is as follows:

∼P → Q ∧ S

Which stated equivalently in the contrapositive form is as follows:

(∼Q ∨ ∼S) → P

To demonstrate the Paul’s assertion is more than apostolic authority we attempt the following two arguments. Propositions will be supported by passages in scripture to ground the arguments in premises the Corinthians themselves would have accepted:

Argument 1: A Direct proof for ∼P → Q ∧ S

let ∼P:

∼P → Death had a hold over him [Acts 2:24 stated in contrapositive]

Death had a hold over him → he was not sinless [undetected]

the result is a contradiction because the Corinthians would have accepted that Christ is sinless [Hebrews 9:14, Exodus 12:5, 1 Peter 1:19]

Conclusion: P is true and therefore Christ did rise from the dead

Argument 2: A proof of the contrapositive (∼Q ∨ ∼S) → P

let (∼Q ∨ ∼S) because ∼S [1 Corinthians 1:2]

(∼Q ∨ ∼S) → your sins were atoned for [by definition; also 1 Corinthians 15:3]

your sins were atoned for → the atoning object was sinless [Exodus 12:5, Hebrews 9:14, 1 Peter 1:19]

the atoning object was sinless → death could not hold it [undetected]

The Problem of Claim 2

Thus the following premise in Argument 1, that:

“Death had a hold over him → he was not sinless”

and its contrapositive in Argument 2, that:

“the atoning object was sinless → death could not hold it”

are not detectable from Scripture.

Tempting Fallacies

Some tempting passages to justify the aforementioned can induce a few logical fallacies:

Genesis 2:17: “… in the day you eat of it you shall surely die”

Statement: If you eat (disobey and hence sin) then you will die

Contrapositive: If you do not die then you did not eat

Inverse: If you do not eat then you will not die

Converse: If you died then you ate (We sin because we will die)

Neither the inverse, which is what I need, nor the converse (which admittedly is a bit funny when reading it out loud) are logically guaranteed; and so cannot be presumed without having been given by the text axiomatically.

Romans 5:12: “sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin.”

Statement: one man’s sin → sin → death

What is allowed: Sin → death

Fallacy: ∼Sin → ∼death ; again unless given

Romans 6:23: “… wages of sin is death.”

Statement: sin → death

Fallacy: ∼Sin → ∼death ; again unless given

It is tempting to argue that “wages” implies an exclusive transactional relationship and so one would be able to argue that: sin ⇔ death (a bi-conditional), so death → sin, and hence ∼sin → ∼death. As tempting as this is, this relies on interpretation as opposed to having been strictly given. But beyond that even granting this interpretation, the other direction of the transaction is the refund: given your life back can you erase your sin? Obviously not.

1 Corinthians 15:56: “the sting of death is sin.” Death’s power comes from sin. Too weak for strict logic.

John 14:30 + Hebrews 2:14: Devil has power of death; devil has no claim on Jesus. But “A controls B” and “A can’t touch C” doesn’t give you “B can’t touch C.”

Acts 2:25–32: “Holy One” won’t see decay. A title, not a deductive chain; i.e. doesn’t say Holy One won’t see decay because he is holy (sinless); to deduce this I need a verse that says Holiness → No decay.

Romans 6:9: “death no longer has dominion over him.” An imperative, not a deductive chain.

Summarizing Comment on Claim 2

The Corinthians accepted Paul’s argument and hence the unstated premise that “sinlessness implies death cannot hold”, and the church has accepted it for over 2000 years. Yet no verse states it, no chain of verses allows it to be deduced, and attempting to deduce it formally fails. The doctrine rests on a claim that every believer makes intuitively but no text supplies explicitly. This points to a limitation of formalism as opposed to a weakness of doctrine. Natural language communicates meaning that formal systems cannot reconstruct from the text alone. The Corinthians accepted this premise based on context, shared assumptions, and theological intuition.

Concluding Synthesis

This exercise demonstrates that formal logic does not map to natural language cleanly but this does not diminish the claim of either to legitimacy. Formal logic remains a powerful tool for reasoning in spaces where meaning is consistent everywhere and every premise is deducible or stated axiomatically. But natural language allows people to arrive at shared conclusions through shared context even when premises are non-deducible. Whether it is the disciples Jesus was speaking to at the last supper or the Corinthians Paul was writing to, both understood something that in both cases cannot be formalized cleanly from the text.

Note: This is an ongoing exercise. The search for the undetected premise remains open, and I welcome any reader who can supply what I could not.
