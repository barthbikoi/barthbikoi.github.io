A Theological Reflection on the Goodness of God and the Problem of Death

A letter to my BIROCOL 2006 Batch

“Woe to him who seeks to pour oil upon the waters when God has brewed them into a gale!”
— Herman Melville, Moby-Dick, Ch. 9

Friends,

I can imagine how difficult it is for everyone now, processing this experience we’re going through. Obviously we’re all experiencing the grief in our unique ways, each according to his degree of closeness to Loïc. I’m not even sure this is the right way to say it because what does “closeness” even mean, when you’ve spent 7 years with someone. Seeing them basically every single day. Or even just 2 years? Not to mention those who continued to be in touch with him up till even the last moments of his life.

When we lost Jude Kum Fung back in Form 1, it was also really sudden by many standards. And most of us were really confused to actually process what happened — at least I was. I haven’t really thought much of him over the years to be frank, until recently because of everything we are going through with Loïc’s death. That was 2006; it’s 2026 now … twenty years that went by to God knows where before we lost another one of us. How many times in these 20 years have I really thought about the reality of death? Not that much to be honest except for when it happens. And even then I tend to forget about it sooner than later.

The most shocking thing about death to me is how indiscriminate it is: it doesn’t care about one’s status or age or whatsoever: it just … happens. And it’s the only thing that will for sure happen in anyone’s life 100%. Yet I tend to be fearful of it or try to distract myself from thinking about the fact that it is inevitable. Sometimes I even dissuade myself from meditating on it by imagining that thinking about it is somehow inviting evil, when the truth is the reality doesn’t change. But beyond all this, what really confounds me about death is the amount of grief that it causes. The type of pain that death causes is different from other types of pain. It doesn’t feel like the pain from a betrayal, or the physical pain from being harmed, nor does it feel like the pain of a romantic heart break. Rather, it is a hopeless sort of pain that comes from accepting, most times subconsciously, just how helpless we are as human beings: we cannot prevent it, predict it (for the most part), or reverse it. And most times it leaves us wondering and regretting why we failed to say and do to the deceased, what we should have done when they were alive.

And to be very frank with you all this has caused me to doubt the goodness of God extremely. What does it even mean when I say “God is good”? How can God who is good also allow something so bitter as the pain of losing a loved one to happen? Not merely the loss itself, but all the emotional wreckage that comes with it? Surely a good God would have found a way to make the burden just a little bit lighter?!

Friends if everything we grew up believing is true then death is the result of our own disobedience to God; a curse for thinking that our own ways are higher than his ways. Still, I would say, fair enough if we accept our beliefs that a Holy God must punish disobedience to also remain a just God.

But I say to you all: wouldn’t a God who loves all his creation surely find a more bearable way to endure the inevitable? Perhaps that everyone would die by falling asleep peacefully and not waking up? Oh… or that those left behind would not be in such suffering? Or maybe even both as long as the death itself happens and thus the penalty is satisfied? Surely a loving God would not allow a woman to lose her husband … and then her son? … nor would a loving God allow a brother to lose his dad … and then his brother? And yet somehow he decided the experience of death was going to be what it is: messy, painful, heart breaking, confounding, unexpected, and all the different things you all are feeling right now that I cannot put in words.

I cannot really tell you guys that right now as I am writing these words I have been able to understand and even resolve this tension in my heart: truly some days I wake up really angry at God. Other days I just give up and tell myself this: if what I believe is actually true, then there is nothing I can do to an Omnipotent being who could blow me away in a millisecond if he so chose to.

But you guys know what frustrates me even more? This evening in the whole atmosphere of mourning our brother Loïc, I just couldn’t stop thinking about chapter 11 of the Gospel of John, that is, the whole thing with Lazarus’ death. Basically two things that stood out and further confused me are as follows: first, when they show up to tell Jesus that if he doesn’t hurry up to save Lazarus he will die, Jesus delays his departure intentionally. Secondly, when he arrives and finds Lazarus dead, Jesus is SO moved at the grief of those mourning that he himself weeps. This to me is insane in the most confusing way! My friends if Jesus is God as we grew up believing then it cannot be that God enjoys seeing us suffering because as we can see in that chapter, God himself in the person of Jesus, cries really hard and is incredibly hurt by just how much pain Mary and her family were in at the death of Lazarus. Crazy! So what is it then. Why does he allow it if he doesn’t derive pleasure from it. Why? The simple truth is I don’t know the answer. And simply quoting Bible verses to you all isn’t going to cut it for most of us, whether because of our own unbelief or because of our suspicion.

But what confuses me even more is the way that this same God decides to save us from this suffering. God doesn’t just stand by and feel bad for us as we saw with Lazarus before raising him back to life: he decides that the only way to deliver us from this once and for all, is by suffering himself. An everlasting God, decides to suffer, to save us from suffering once and for all.

My friends if what we believe is true, then God decided to save us by becoming incarnate, living the perfect life and satisfy his eternal law in a way that we could never do because the human heart is sinful in its very nature. And then he goes to the cross to die for our sins against him, taking the punishment that we deserved and exchanging his righteous record for us so that anyone anywhere who would call upon the name of the Lord would be saved from his eternal anger in hell, foreshadowed by the death and suffering we experience in this world. A God, infinitely beautiful and magnificent. Becoming a creature to save his creatures out of his infinite love for each and everyone of us. My friends this in my opinion, is the only possible explanation, after thinking seriously about the goodness of God and the prevalence of death and evil in the world, of what we really mean when we say that God is good. Because no other explanation makes any sense. It just can’t. Any other explanation will either collapse into an irony or a paradox.

Why God chose to be good to us in this specific way is something that I don’t understand. And if I’m being honest isn’t something I will ever understand until the time comes for him to reveal all things to us. But if what I grew up being taught is true, it seems to me to be the only way that what I believe actually makes any sense. And it would therefore also imply that my understanding of what it means for God to be good was completely different from what the Lord understands to be good.

Therefore my brothers let us renounce every hope of finding security in anything that we own in this world and ask the Lord himself to cause us to trust in him for our eternal salvation. Because if what we grew up believing is true, and if Loïc trusted in our Lord to be saved, then we will see him again on that day. Not maybe, but definitely. And on that day when the Lord returns, wouldn’t the sweetest thing we anticipate be what he tells us in the book of Revelation? That he himself will “wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away”?

Until then we live in this world and we wrestle. And we ask the Lord for the humbleness of heart to seek his refuge apart from which there is no hope. Take courage my brothers, the Lord himself will be with us.

With all solemnity, and until the Lord returns, I remain, truly,
Njiki Bikoi
